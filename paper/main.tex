\begin{abstract}
Brain-to-image decoders combine brain-derived features with strong generative
priors and stochastic sampling, so a realistic reconstruction does not by itself
show whether brain activity shaped it. We study the \textbf{Degree of Brain
Control (BC)}: the ratio of the within-stimulus variance of decoder outputs
under shuffled versus preserved trial--stimulus correspondence, computed over
repeated presentations of the same stimuli. BC is a multivariate reliability
measure. It is an affine function of the one-way ANOVA $F$ statistic and equals
$1/(1-\mathrm{I2C2})$ for the method-of-moments estimate of the image intraclass
correlation coefficient. BC exceeds 1 when stimulus-specific structure reaches
the output, and unlike $F$ it does not grow with the number of repetitions.
Because it needs only trial labels, BC can be computed in any representation of
a pipeline. For generative decoders we add two decompositions: one separates
trial-to-trial from sampling-driven variability across diffusion seeds, the
other separates the part explained by a semantic clustering of the stimuli from
the rest.

On the GOD dataset, BC computed on decoded features separates real from
shuffled fMRI input (1.20 vs.\ 1.00, mean over five subjects), which
reference-free statistics such as the feature norm cannot. On the Natural
Scenes Dataset, single-trial reconstructions of MindEye2 (four subjects),
MindEye1 and Brain-Diffuser (subject 1) all show reproducible,
stimulus-specific variation (BC 1.25--1.87 in DreamSim space; shuffled controls
0.99--1.01). In MindEye2 (subject 1), 40 semantic clusters explain about 45\% of
the excess over 1 in the final images, and 55--63\% of the within-stimulus
variance of a single reconstruction comes from the diffusion seed. BC ranks the
four decoders in the same order as pairwise identification accuracy but spaces
them differently. BC does not measure how much of an image comes from the brain
rather than from the prior. We propose it as a calibrated reliability
diagnostic that complements identification accuracy and separates stimulus,
trial, seed and category contributions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Brain-to-image reconstruction has progressed from simple contrast patterns and
coarse reconstructions of natural movies \citep{miyawaki2008,nishimoto2011} to
producing images that appear to reflect fine-grained visual content
\citep{shen2019,ozcelik2023,scotti2023,scotti2024}. This progress has been
driven in large part by increasingly powerful generative models---GANs, VAEs and
diffusion models---that serve as the image synthesis stage of reconstruction
pipelines. Reconstructions are evaluated primarily by their similarity to the
presented stimulus: pixel-level fidelity, perceptual similarity or
identification accuracy. By these measures, recent methods appear highly
successful.

Yet similarity to the stimulus does not say how much of a reconstruction was
determined by brain activity. Modern pipelines contain three sources of
structure: the brain signal, the generative prior and random sampling. A
generator with a strong prior can produce realistic, category-consistent images
from inputs that carry little stimulus-specific information, and a diffusion
sampler produces a different image every time it is run on the same input.
\citet{shirakawa2025} argued that the apparent realism of recent text-guided
reconstructions on the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) \citep{allen2022} stems from
classification into trained categories combined with hallucination by diffusion
models rather than from genuine visual reconstruction. Statistics that need no
reference---visual plausibility or
statistics of the decoded features---cannot tell whether brain activity
determined the output. Reference-based metrics such as identification accuracy
can reveal that a reconstruction is unrelated to its stimulus, but they report
similarity to a target rather than how strongly brain activity constrains the
output, and they require a ground-truth representation in the space being
evaluated.

Repeated presentations of the same stimulus give a direct handle on this
question. If brain activity determines the output, reconstructions from
different trials of the same stimulus should be more similar to each other than
reconstructions from trials of different stimuli. We quantify this with the
\textbf{Degree of Brain Control (BC)}, the ratio of the within-stimulus variance
of decoder outputs when the trial--stimulus correspondence is broken to the same
variance when it is preserved. $\mathrm{BC}=1$ when the outputs carry no
stimulus information, whatever their visual quality. With the same number of
trials per stimulus, BC is an affine function of the one-way ANOVA $F$
statistic, i.e.\ the Calinski--Harabasz index \citep{calinski1974}, and is
closely related to the intraclass correlation (ICC) \citep{shrout1979}. BC is
therefore not a new statistic: it is a multivariate reliability measure, and
for a standard estimator it is an exact transform of the image intraclass
correlation coefficient (I2C2) \citep{shou2013}. Our contribution is to apply
it to generative fMRI-to-image decoders at the level of single trials, and to
decompose it into the sources of variability specific to these decoders.

BC and identification accuracy answer different questions. Identification
accuracy asks whether a reconstruction is closer to its own stimulus than to
other stimuli; BC asks whether reconstructions of the same stimulus agree with
each other more than reconstructions of different stimuli. In the language of
measurement, BC is a reliability measure and identification accuracy a measure
of accuracy with respect to a target. A decoder that turns every presentation of
a stimulus into the same wrong image is consistent but not accurate. Because the
decoder is fixed and the sampling noise does not depend on the stimulus,
structure that is consistent across presentations of a stimulus must have
reached the output through the brain signal, whether or not it is correct.

BC does not measure how much of the output's content comes from the brain
rather than from the generative prior. Consider a decoder that reads only the
category of a stimulus from the brain and then outputs a fixed prototype image
of that category. Its outputs are nearly identical across presentations, so its
BC is very high, yet every detail of the image---texture, pose,
background---comes from the generator. A stable, brain-driven choice and
extensive completion by the prior can coexist; BC detects the former and is
silent about the latter.

We make the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A calibrated, label-only reliability measure.} We define BC and
    show that it is an affine function of $F$, equals $1/(1-\mathrm{I2C2})$ for
    the method-of-moments estimator and approximates $1/(1-\mathrm{ICC})$, and
    that, unlike $F$, its scale does not depend on the number of trials.
    Because BC uses only trial labels, it can be computed in any representation
    of a pipeline, including spaces without a ground-truth target.

  \item \textbf{Decompositions for stochastic generative decoders.} A nested
    variance decomposition across diffusion seeds separates trial-to-trial from
    sampling-driven variability and gives BC as a function of the number of
    samples averaged. A category-controlled variant separates the part of BC
    explained by a semantic clustering of the stimuli from the rest, addressing
    part of the concern raised by \citet{shirakawa2025}.

  \item \textbf{Validation under controlled inputs (GOD).} Real fMRI input
    yields $\mathrm{BC}>1$ in all five subjects, while shuffled input, whose
    decoded features have the same norms, and random input yield
    $\mathrm{BC}=1.00$. BC carries the same
    information as the Calinski--Harabasz index but, unlike it, is stable when
    the number of trials changes.

  \item \textbf{A trial-level audit of current decoders (NSD).} We measure BC at
    four pipeline stages of MindEye2 in four subjects, and in the final images of
    MindEye1 and Brain-Diffuser, with predictions and analysis plans written
    down in advance (\cref{sec:nsd-setup}). Single-trial reconstructions of all
    methods show reproducible, stimulus-specific variation; reducing MindEye2's subject-specific training data
    from 40 sessions to 1 lowers final-image BC in every subject; and, in
    subject 1, about 45\% of the final-image BC excess is explained by 40
    semantic clusters and more than half of the within-stimulus variability of
    one reconstruction comes from the sampling seed.

  \item \textbf{An explicit comparison with identification accuracy.} BC and
    pairwise identification accuracy order the four decoders identically; across
    subjects they disagree only for two subjects whose values are close on both
    measures. They space the decoders differently, and this difference is not
    explained by the ceiling of identification accuracy alone (exploratory). We
    found no case in which BC would change a ranking of decoders obtained from
    identification accuracy. What BC adds is a calibrated scale, a stage-wise
    view, and the separation of brain, category and sampling contributions.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{sec:related} reviews related work. \Cref{sec:method} defines BC and its
decompositions. \Cref{sec:god} validates BC on GOD, and \cref{sec:nsd} applies
it to NSD decoders. \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and
limitations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Brain-to-Image Reconstruction}

Early work reconstructed simple contrast patterns by combining local image
decoders \citep{miyawaki2008}, and natural movies by combining motion-energy
encoding models with an empirical prior of natural movie clips
\citep{nishimoto2011}.
\citet{horikawa2017} decoded features of a deep convolutional network from
brain activity, introducing the Generic Object Decoding (GOD) dataset, and
\citet{shen2019} reconstructed images from decoded features, optionally
constraining them with a deep generator network trained on natural images.

Subsequent work moved to more powerful generative backbones and to NSD, which
contains 7T fMRI responses of eight subjects to tens of thousands of natural
images \citep{allen2022}. Brain-Diffuser \citep{ozcelik2023} regresses VDVAE
latents \citep{child2021} and CLIP embeddings \citep{radford2021} from fMRI and
generates images with Versatile Diffusion \citep{xu2023vd}. MindEye
\citep{scotti2023} maps fMRI to CLIP image embeddings with contrastive learning
and a diffusion prior. MindEye2 \citep{scotti2024} pretrains a shared-subject
model so that a new subject can be decoded from as little as one session of
data, and generates images with an unCLIP model fine-tuned from SDXL
\citep{podell2024}. MinD-Vis \citep{chen2023} uses masked brain modeling before
diffusion-based synthesis. These methods share a structural property: a
generative prior and, in most cases, a stochastic sampler sit between the
brain-decoded input and the final image. In VAEs \citep{kingma2014}, GANs
\citep{goodfellow2014} and diffusion models \citep{ho2020,song2021} alike, the
generator is trained to produce outputs on the natural-image manifold, so
stronger priors may yield more realistic reconstructions at the cost of
reflecting individual brain states less. This motivates assessing
stimulus-specific reliability alongside reconstruction accuracy and perceptual
quality.

\subsection{Evaluating Reconstructions}

\textbf{Pixel-level metrics} (MSE, SSIM, PSNR) compare reconstructed and target
images pixel by pixel. They are poorly suited to generative pipelines whose
outputs lie on a natural-image manifold regardless of input quality.
\textbf{Perceptual similarity metrics} such as LPIPS \citep{zhang2018} and
DreamSim \citep{fu2023} measure distances in deep feature spaces and better
reflect human judgments of how similar a reconstruction \textit{looks} to the
target. \textbf{Identification accuracy} (top-$k$ or pairwise) tests whether a
reconstruction is closer to its target than to distractors and has become a
standard benchmark \citep{shen2019,scotti2023}. It falls to chance when the
output is unrelated to the stimulus, so it does detect a decoder that ignores
brain activity, but it requires a ground-truth representation of the stimulus,
its scale depends on the distractor set, and it saturates as decoders improve.

\textbf{Spurious reconstruction.} \citet{shirakawa2025} found that recent
text-guided reconstruction methods trained on NSD generalize poorly to a
different dataset, that NSD training and test images overlap in semantic and
visual clusters, and concluded that the apparent realism of these
reconstructions stems from classification into trained categories combined with
hallucination by diffusion models. These two concerns motivate our
category-controlled BC (\cref{sec:beyond}), which asks how much of the measured
brain control remains once coarse semantic categories are accounted for, and
our seed decomposition (\cref{sec:seed}), which measures how much of the
within-stimulus variability of a reconstruction comes from the sampler rather
than from differences between trials.
Neither analysis tests generalization to categories absent from training.

\textbf{Variance-ratio and reliability statistics.} The ratio of between- to
within-group variance underlies the one-way ANOVA $F$ statistic, the
Calinski--Harabasz index of cluster validity \citep{calinski1974} and the ICC
used to assess the reliability of repeated measurements \citep{shrout1979}. The
closest prior work is the image intraclass correlation coefficient (I2C2) of
\citet{shou2013}, which measures the reliability of replicated images with
traces of covariance matrices, obtains confidence intervals by resampling
subjects and builds a null distribution by permuting labels. With stimuli in
place of subjects, BC is an exact transform of its method-of-moments estimate
(\cref{sec:anova}). Our contribution is to apply this kind of reliability
measure to generative fMRI-to-image decoders, to extend it to the sources of
variability specific to them, and to audit current pipelines at the level of
single trials.

\section{Method: Degree of Brain Control}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Setup}

Let $\mathbf{b}_i$ denote the fMRI response on trial $i$, and let
$s_i\in\{1,\ldots,C\}$ denote the stimulus presented on that trial. Each of the
$C$ stimuli is presented $n$ times, so there are $N=nC$ trials. A reconstruction
pipeline maps each trial through a sequence of representations---for example
decoded features, a diffusion prior's output and the final image---and we write
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$ for the representation of trial $i$ at the
stage being evaluated. For images, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$ is an embedding of the
reconstructed image (DreamSim or CLIP). Let $S_c=\{i:s_i=c\}$ be the trials of
stimulus $c$.

In GOD, each test image is the only test image of its object category, so
``stimulus'' and ``category'' coincide; in NSD, the unit is the individual image.
Throughout, BC is computed from single-trial outputs: trials are never averaged
before decoding.

\subsection{Definition}

We define two variance quantities over $\{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i\}$.

\textbf{Within-stimulus variance under preserved correspondence}
($V_\mathrm{pres}$) measures trial-to-trial variability among trials of the same
stimulus:
\begin{equation}
  V_\mathrm{pres}
  = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}
    \mathrm{Var}_{i\in S_c}[\hat{x}_{ik}].
\end{equation}

\textbf{Within-stimulus variance under broken correspondence}
($V_\mathrm{brok}$) measures the same quantity after randomly permuting the
trial indices, destroying the trial--stimulus pairing:
\begin{equation}
  V_\mathrm{brok}
  = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[
    \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}
    \mathrm{Var}_{i\in S_c}[\hat{x}_{\pi(i),k}]
  \right],
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ is a uniformly random permutation of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$.

The \textbf{Degree of Brain Control} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
    \mathrm{BC} = \frac{V_\mathrm{brok}}{V_\mathrm{pres}}
  }
  \label{eq:bc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Interpretation}

\textbf{$\mathrm{BC}=1$} (no mean-level stimulus dependence): Shuffling trial
indices does not change the variance structure of the outputs, because the mean
output does not differ between stimuli. If the outputs carry no stimulus
information, $\mathrm{BC}=1$ in the population, but the converse does not hold
in general: BC captures differences in the mean output, so stimulus information
carried only by the spread or shape of the output distribution leaves BC at 1.
In practice, $\mathrm{BC}\approx1$ means that no mean-level separation by
stimulus is detected in the chosen representation.

\textbf{$\mathrm{BC}>1$}: Trials of the same stimulus produce more similar
outputs than randomly paired trials. Because the decoder is fixed and only its
brain input depends on the stimulus, this stimulus-specific structure has
reached the output through the brain signal. $\mathrm{BC}>1$ does not say how
much of the output's content is determined by the brain rather than by the
generator.

The quantity $\mathrm{BC}-1$ serves as an effect size of stimulus-specific
consistency. If,
for each stimulus, output dimension $k$ is Gaussian with a variance shared
across stimuli, BC bounds the mutual information between the stimulus label and
that dimension:
\begin{equation}
  I(s;\,\hat{x}_k)
  \le \frac{1}{2}\log\mathrm{BC}_k,
  \label{eq:mi}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{BC}_k$ is the population value of BC computed on dimension $k$
alone. The bound holds because a Gaussian has maximal entropy for a given
variance. For the full vector the corresponding bound involves determinants of
covariance matrices rather than traces, so $\frac{1}{2}\log\mathrm{BC}$ should
not be read as the mutual information of the whole vector.

\subsection{Relationship to \texorpdfstring{$F$}{F}, the Calinski--Harabasz Index and the ICC}
\label{sec:anova}

Let $SS_W$, $SS_B$ and $SS_T=SS_W+SS_B$ denote the within-stimulus,
between-stimulus and total sums of squares of the outputs, summed over
dimensions. The sample variance of a subset drawn without replacement is
unbiased for the finite-population variance. With $n$ trials per stimulus, the
permutation expectation in \cref{eq:bc} therefore has a closed form,
$V_\mathrm{brok}=SS_T/(d(N-1))$ and $V_\mathrm{pres}=SS_W/(d(N-C))$, so that
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BC}
  = \frac{N-C}{N-1}\cdot\frac{SS_T}{SS_W}
  = 1 + \frac{C-1}{N-1}\,(F-1),
  \qquad
  F = \frac{SS_B/(C-1)}{SS_W/(N-C)}.
  \label{eq:anova}
\end{equation}
$F$ is the one-way ANOVA $F$ statistic computed on traces, which equals the
Calinski--Harabasz index \citep{calinski1974} of the outputs with stimuli as
clusters. BC therefore carries the same information as $F$; permutations are
needed only for a $p$-value.

Writing the one-way random-effects variance components as
$\sigma_w^2=SS_W/(N-C)$ and $\sigma_s^2=(SS_B/(C-1)-\sigma_w^2)/n$ (both summed
over dimensions), \cref{eq:anova} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BC} = 1 + \frac{n(C-1)}{N-1}\cdot\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_w^2}
  \;\approx\; 1 + \frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_w^2}
  = \frac{1}{1-\mathrm{ICC}},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{ICC} = \frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_s^2+\sigma_w^2},
  \label{eq:icc}
\end{equation}
where the approximation uses $n(C-1)/(N-1)\to 1$ for many stimuli (it is 0.999
for $C=500$, $n=3$). $\mathrm{BC}-1$ is thus the ratio of stimulus-driven to
within-stimulus variance, and the ICC is the corresponding share of the total.
The two scales differ in emphasis: the ICC is bounded by 1, whereas $\mathrm{BC}-1$
grows without bound as stimulus-driven variance dominates.

The relation is exact for the image intraclass correlation coefficient (I2C2) of
\citet{shou2013}, defined as $1-\mathrm{tr}K_U/\mathrm{tr}K_W$ with $K_U$ the
within-subject and $K_W$ the total covariance of replicated images. Its
method-of-moments estimator uses $\mathrm{tr}\hat K_W=SS_T/(N-1)$ and
$\mathrm{tr}\hat K_U=SS_W/(N-C)$, which with stimuli in place of subjects and
\cref{eq:anova} gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BC}=\frac{1}{1-\hat\rho_\mathrm{I2C2}}.
\end{equation}

$F$ and BC also differ in scale with respect to $n$. For a fixed ratio of
between- to within-stimulus variance, $F$ grows roughly in proportion to $n$,
whereas BC approaches $1+\sigma_s^2/\sigma_w^2$ and does not depend on $n$. BC
relates to $F$ as an effect size relates to its test statistic; we verify this
empirically in \cref{sec:god-metrics}.

\subsection{Relationship to Identification Accuracy}
\label{sec:ident-theory}

Both BC and identification accuracy increase as stimulus-driven variance grows
relative to within-stimulus variance, so we expect them to rank decoders
similarly. They differ in three respects. First, they measure different
properties. BC measures the consistency of outputs across presentations of the
same stimulus; identification accuracy measures how close outputs are to a
ground-truth representation of the stimulus. The two are related, but neither
determines the other. A decoder that
maps every presentation of a stimulus to the same wrong output has high BC and
chance-level identification; conversely, within-stimulus variability in
directions that do not affect the comparison with the target lowers BC without
lowering identification accuracy. BC is computed from squared Euclidean
distances, whereas identification accuracy here uses correlations, so the two
also weight directions of the embedding space differently. Second, identification accuracy is
bounded and approaches its ceiling nonlinearly, whereas $\mathrm{BC}-1$ is
linear in $\sigma_s^2/\sigma_w^2$. Third, identification accuracy requires a
ground-truth representation of the stimulus in the evaluated space; BC requires
only the trial labels.

\subsection{Category-Controlled BC}
\label{sec:beyond}

Trials of the same stimulus also share its semantic category, so $\mathrm{BC}>1$
can arise from category-level information alone. To separate the two, we group
stimuli into $G$ clusters (here, k-means on CLIP embeddings of the ground-truth
images) and fit a nested ANOVA with stimuli nested in clusters. With
$\sigma_{s|g}^2=(MS_{S|G}-MS_W)/n$, the stimulus variance within clusters, we
define
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BC}_\text{beyond}
  = 1 + \frac{n(C-1)}{N-1}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{s|g}^2}{\sigma_w^2},
  \qquad
  \text{category share} = \frac{\mathrm{BC}-\mathrm{BC}_\text{beyond}}{\mathrm{BC}-1}.
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{BC}_\text{beyond}\approx1$ when the mean output depends on the stimulus
only through its cluster. $\mathrm{BC}_\text{beyond}>1$ shows stimulus-specific
consistency that the chosen clustering does not explain; finer semantic
distinctions within a cluster can account for part of it.

\subsection{Separating Trial-to-Trial from Sampling-Driven Variability}
\label{sec:seed}

In a diffusion-based pipeline, two trials of the same stimulus differ both
because the measured responses differ (neural variability, measurement noise and
preprocessing) and because the sampler draws different noise. To separate these, we generate each trial with $R$ seeds and fit a nested
ANOVA (stimulus $>$ trial $>$ seed) with mean squares $MS_A$, $MS_B$, $MS_E$:
$\sigma_e^2=MS_E$ (seed), $\sigma_b^2=(MS_B-MS_E)/R$ (trial) and
$\sigma_a^2=(MS_A-MS_B)/(nR)$ (stimulus). BC for outputs averaged over $k$ seeds
per trial is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BC}(k)
  = 1 + \frac{n(C-1)}{N-1}\cdot\frac{\sigma_a^2}{\sigma_b^2+\sigma_e^2/k}.
  \label{eq:bck}
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{BC}(1)$ corresponds to the usual single-sample setting.
$\mathrm{BC}(\infty)$ removes sampling variability; it corresponds to embeddings
averaged over infinitely many seeds and is not attained by any single image. The
\textbf{sampling share} $\sigma_e^2/(\sigma_b^2+\sigma_e^2)$ is the fraction of
the within-stimulus variance of one sample that comes from the seed. The
decomposition separates sampling variability from trial-to-trial variability.
It does not isolate the contribution of the learned prior, which also shapes
the content that is reproduced across seeds.

\subsection{Computation and Uncertainty}
\label{sec:computation}

When every stimulus has the same number of trials, we compute BC with the closed
form in \cref{eq:anova}. Wherever we computed both (GOD, and stages S0, S3 and
the token-mean S1 and S2 of MindEye2 in NSD subject 1), the closed form matched
1,000-permutation estimates to within 0.0003. As a shuffled control we permute the stimulus labels
across trials and recompute BC; by construction it should be close to 1.
Confidence intervals come from a stimulus-level bootstrap (1,000 resamples of
stimuli, each with all of its trials). Comparisons between decoders on the same
stimuli use the same resampling weights (paired bootstrap). Because a resampled
stimulus that appears twice is counted as two stimuli, the bootstrap
distribution of BC is biased low (by about $1.3(\mathrm{BC}-1)/C$ in
simulations); we therefore shift the percentile interval by the difference
between the point estimate and the bootstrap mean. In the GOD experiments
(\cref{sec:god}), BC is the 1,000-permutation estimate and, where stated, is
computed per category and averaged.

\section{Validation on GOD: Controlled Inputs}
\label{sec:god}

\subsection{Setup}

We use the Generic Object Decoding (GOD) dataset \citep{horikawa2017}, which
contains fMRI responses from five subjects viewing 1,200 training images and 50
test images (one image per category, repeated 35 times each). Test trials thus
consist of $50\times35=1{,}750$ trials per subject. All analyses use the Visual
Cortex (VC) ROI unless otherwise stated. We train a Ridge regression decoder
($\alpha=100$) with standard scaling to predict DNN features from brain signals.
The main analyses use the \texttt{cnn8} features distributed with GOD (final
fully connected layer of an AlexNet-type network, $d=1{,}000$). The ROI analysis
and the reconstructed images use AlexNet relu7 features ($d=4{,}096$), which
serve as input to the relu7generator; these were extracted for the 733 of 1,200
training images available to us, so the corresponding decoders are trained on
those 733 images.

We compare three conditions that differ only in the brain signals fed to the
decoder: \textbf{Real} (genuine test signals), \textbf{Shuffled} (test trial
indices randomly permuted before decoding, preserving the marginal distribution
of brain signals) and \textbf{Random} (standard Gaussian noise in place of brain
signals). The decoder and generator are identical across conditions.

\subsection{Real versus Shuffled Input}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{BC, feature norm and pairwise identification accuracy across three
  input conditions (Subject 1, VC ROI, \texttt{cnn8} features).}
\label{tab:main}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Condition & BC & Feature norm (L2) & Identification accuracy \\
\midrule
Real      & \textbf{1.259} & 70.9  & \textbf{0.726} \\
Shuffled  & 1.001          & 70.9  & 0.500 \\
Random    & 1.000          & 118.4 & 0.490 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Real and Shuffled conditions produce decoded features with identical L2 norms
(\cref{tab:main})---the shuffled set contains the same decoded vectors in a
different order---so no statistic of the decoded features alone can distinguish
them. BC separates them sharply (1.259 vs.\ 1.001), as does identification
accuracy against the true stimulus features (0.726 vs.\ 0.500, chance 0.5).
\Cref{fig:recon} shows that the Real and Shuffled reconstructions are visually
indistinguishable in quality. Real BC exceeds Shuffled BC in every one of the
five subjects (\cref{tab:multisubj}).

These BC values describe the decoded \texttt{cnn8} features, not the images in
\cref{fig:recon}, which come from a separate relu7 pipeline. In the generated
images themselves, consistency is much weaker: on DreamSim embeddings of the

\section{Application to NSD: Single-Trial Reconstructions of Current Decoders}
\label{sec:nsd}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:nsd-setup}

\textbf{Data.} We use the shared test images of NSD \citep{allen2022}, each
viewed three times by each subject ($n=3$). Every analysis uses single-trial
fMRI responses; the three trials are never averaged.

\textbf{MindEye2.} We use the public MindEye2 models \citep{scotti2024} for
subjects 1, 2, 5 and 7. Both are fine-tuned after shared-subject pretraining,
on either all 40 sessions (\textbf{40sess}) or one session (\textbf{1sess}) of
the subject's data. We record four stages: \textbf{S0}, the subject-specific ridge
output (4,096 dimensions); \textbf{S1}, the backbone output that conditions the
diffusion prior ($256\times1{,}664$); \textbf{S2}, the diffusion prior output
that conditions unCLIP ($256\times1{,}664$); and \textbf{S3}, the final unCLIP
image ($256\times256$, without the refinement stage), embedded with DreamSim
\citep{fu2023} (1,792 dimensions) and CLIP ViT-L/14 \citep{radford2021} (768
dimensions). For S1 and S2 we also report BC on the mean over the 256 tokens.
For CLIP we use the OpenAI weights with the QuickGELU activation they were
trained with. Our first analysis of subject 1 had loaded them with the standard
GELU by mistake; we noticed this from a library warning after that analysis and
recomputed all CLIP embeddings. DreamSim was not affected.

\textbf{MindEye1 and Brain-Diffuser.} For subject 1 we also run MindEye1
\citep{scotti2023} with its public weights (voxel-to-CLIP mapping, diffusion
prior and low-level pathway) and Versatile Diffusion \citep{xu2023vd}, and
Brain-Diffuser \citep{ozcelik2023}. Brain-Diffuser's regression weights are not
public, so we retrained its ridge regressions on the original training data
(8,859 training images, with the trials of each image averaged as in the
original code; 15,724 voxels), using a closed-form implementation that matches
scikit-learn's ridge regression to within $10^{-13}$ (checked on the first 768
output dimensions), and applied them to single-trial responses. As in the
original code, the predictions are standardized with their mean and standard
deviation over all test predictions and rescaled to the statistics of the
training targets. We
generate one image per trial with a fixed per-trial seed and do not select among
several samples (MindEye1 originally selects the best of 16 samples with CLIP,
which would change BC).

\textbf{Reproduction checks.} Before the main runs we checked that our
pipelines reproduce the published ones. For MindEye2, we reconstructed ten test
stimuli per subject with the 40sess model; for every one of them, the closest
published reconstruction (CLIP) was that of the same stimulus. For MindEye1 and
Brain-Diffuser, reconstructions from three-trial averages (100 images) reached
CLIP two-way identification accuracies of 94.6\% and 92.6\%, close to the
94.6\% and 92.5\% reported for subject 1 (on the full test sets of the original
papers, so the comparison is approximate). We ported the official CUDA code to PyTorch's MPS
backend (float16 autocast, attention without xformers, VAE decoding in float32).
MindEye2 checkpoints were loaded with strict key matching; for MindEye1 (after
dropping keys used only in training and renaming attention keys) and for
Brain-Diffuser's Versatile Diffusion checkpoint, we verified that no weights
were missing or unexpected.

\textbf{Stimuli.} Stimuli are processed in a fixed random order (seed 42). For
MindEye2, S0--S2 were computed for all 1,000 shared images and S3 for the first
500 ($K=500$, a random sample), the same 500 images for all four subjects.
Unless stated otherwise, all stages are reported on these 500 images. MindEye1 and
Brain-Diffuser, whose original code uses the publicly released sessions 1--37,
are evaluated on the 839 of their 982 test images that have three trials in
those sessions; the comparison across methods uses the 414 images that are
among the first 500 of every method.

\textbf{Statistics.} BC uses the closed form (\cref{eq:anova}), with shuffled
controls and stimulus-level bootstrap intervals (\cref{sec:computation}).
Pairwise identification accuracy correlates each S3 embedding with the
ground-truth embeddings of all evaluated images and reports the fraction of
distractors ranked below the true image (chance 0.5).

\textbf{Predictions.} We wrote down predictions and the analysis plan in
advance: for the MindEye2 experiments before the images were generated, and for
the category analysis before it was run. For the method comparison we made no
predictions about differences between methods; its two checks (shuffled control
close to 1, lower CI bound above 1) were planned before generation, while the
tolerance of the first ($\pm0.05$) and the analysis details were fixed after
generation but before the results were examined. These plans were not externally registered. We state
below which predictions failed.

\textbf{Final images show stimulus-specific consistency.} In every subject and for
both models, S3 BC is well above 1 (lowest lower CI bound 1.207, subject 7,
1sess, CLIP) while the shuffled control stays at 1. Single-trial MindEye2
reconstructions therefore carry substantial stimulus-specific structure.

\textbf{Less subject-specific training data lowers BC.} The 1sess model has
lower S3 BC than the 40sess model in all four subjects (differences +0.26 to
+0.41 in DreamSim, all CIs excluding 0). The ratio of $\mathrm{BC}-1$ between the
two models is 1.8--2.1 in every subject. At the ridge stage the difference is
small or absent (+0.063 and +0.168 in subjects 1 and 2; $-0.001$ and +0.004,
with CIs including 0, in subjects 5 and 7), whereas it is larger at S1 (+0.21
to +0.47), in the token-mean S1 and S2 (+0.32 to +0.83) and at S3. This pattern
was not predicted and is exploratory. Because the differences at different
stages are measured in different representations, it is descriptive; it is
consistent with subject-specific data acting mainly after the ridge stage,
through the shared backbone.

\textbf{BC across stages.} We had predicted that BC would decrease from S1 to S2 to S3. With
token means, it does so in all four subjects and both models (e.g.\ subject 1,
40sess: 2.82, 2.46, 1.87). In full dimension the prediction fails: S2 has the
lowest BC of all stages (1.09--1.28), below S3. Sampling contributes most of the
within-stimulus variance of the full-dimensional S2, but S2 stays below S3 even
when sampling is removed from both (\cref{sec:nsd-seed}). BC depends on the
representation: it is not invariant to rescaling individual dimensions, and
averaging over tokens changes it, as the full-dimensional and token-mean S2
show. Differences between stages therefore describe consistency in each
representation and do not by themselves locate where stimulus information is
lost. Comparisons between models at the same stage are the most direct.

\textbf{Subjects differ from the first stage.} Subject 7 has the lowest BC at
every stage, starting at S0 (1.445 vs.\ 1.77--1.90 for the other three),
suggesting that the difference originates in the input signal or the ridge
mapping rather than in the generative stages (exploratory).

To address the concern that reconstructions reflect only the semantic category

models (S3 DreamSim: 1.47 for 40sess, 1.27 for 1sess; shuffled 0.987--0.998),
and its lower CI bound is above 1.12 for every stage, model and $k$.
MindEye2's single-trial reconstructions therefore show stimulus-specific
consistency that the 40 clusters do not explain; finer semantic distinctions

19--33\% at the ridge and backbone stages (full dimension). The category share grows with $k$ (0.15--0.42

distractors from the same cluster is lower than overall identification (S3
DreamSim: 0.905 vs.\ 0.982 for 40sess; 0.765 vs.\ 0.902 for 1sess), consistent
with part of the overall accuracy coming from category discrimination. The
difference between 40sess and 1sess persists beyond categories (+0.195 in S3
DreamSim). We had predicted a larger category share for 1sess at the prior and
image stages. This was not supported: the share was similar (S2, 0.29 vs.\
0.27) or smaller (token-mean S2 and S3). At the ridge and backbone stages, which
the prediction did not cover, it was larger for 1sess (0.27 vs.\ 0.19 and 0.33
vs.\ 0.22; exploratory).

Because unCLIP and the diffusion prior are stochastic, the three trials of a

comes from the seed} (sampling share 0.55--0.63 at S3, similar for both models).
Consequently, $\mathrm{BC}(1)-1$ is only 37--45\% of $\mathrm{BC}(\infty)-1$: S3
DreamSim BC rises from 1.84 to 2.92 (40sess) and from 1.43 to 1.97 (1sess) when

and 1.74. The difference between the models remains when sampling is removed
(BC($\infty$), DreamSim: +0.95 [+0.68, +1.23]). Since the sampling share is the

variance relative to the within-stimulus variance, not a larger share of
sampling noise.

seed (0.21--0.23 after token averaging), and removing sampling raises its BC
from 1.27 to 2.04 (40sess) and from 1.20 to 1.74 (1sess). Even so, it stays
below S3 with sampling removed (40sess, 100 images: 2.07 vs.\ 2.92), so the
failed prediction of \cref{sec:nsd-stages} is not only a matter of sampling. We
had also predicted that sampling would explain most of the gap between
full-dimensional and token-mean S2 (subject 1, 1,000 images: 1.157 for 40sess
and 0.557 for 1sess). The plan did not specify how to measure this. Removing
sampling from both narrows the gap by only 34\% [32\%, 36\%] (40sess) and 56\%
[52\%, 59\%] (1sess), whereas removing it from the full-dimensional S2 alone
closes 67\% [65\%, 69\%] and 97\% [94\%, 101\%] of the gap; we regard the
prediction as only partly supported.

\subsection{BC and Identification Accuracy}
\label{sec:nsd-ident}

Across the four decoders, BC and pairwise identification accuracy give the same
ranking---MindEye2 40sess $>$ MindEye1 $>$ Brain-Diffuser $>$ MindEye2 1sess---in
both embedding spaces, and all six paired differences exclude 0 for both
measures (\cref{tab:methoddiff}). Across subjects, the two measures order
subjects 2 and 7 identically for both models and in both spaces; the only
disagreements concern subjects 1 and 5, whose values are close on both measures
(differences of at most 0.04 in BC and 0.02 in identification accuracy;
\cref{tab:subjects} and \cref{fig:stages}B). We did not compute intervals for differences between
subjects. We found no case in which the two measures order two decoders
differently.

The two measures space the decoders differently (exploratory, not predicted).
MindEye2 40sess and MindEye1 differ by 1.3 points of DreamSim identification
accuracy but by 0.195 in BC, whereas Brain-Diffuser and MindEye2 1sess differ by
5.6 points of identification accuracy but by only 0.063 in BC. The ceiling of
identification accuracy does not explain this by itself. After a probit
transform, which approximately linearizes pairwise accuracy under a Gaussian
model, the second difference is still larger than the first (0.43 vs.\ 0.22 in
DreamSim space; 0.43 vs.\ 0.08 in CLIP space; computed post hoc from
\cref{tab:methods}), whereas in BC it is smaller (0.063 vs.\ 0.195; 0.079 vs.\
0.125). The probit is on a $d'$-like scale, which grows with the square root of
the variance ratio, while $\mathrm{BC}-1$ grows with the ratio itself, but the
reversal remains on matched scales ($\sqrt{\mathrm{BC}-1}$ differences 0.044
vs.\ 0.112 in DreamSim space and 0.058 vs.\ 0.073 in CLIP space) and with a
logit transform of accuracy (0.90 vs.\ 0.54 in DreamSim space). Brain-Diffuser
is thus much better identified than MindEye2 1sess but only slightly more
consistent across presentations. We do not attribute this to a single cause:
the pipelines differ, and BC and identification accuracy weight directions of
the embedding space differently (\cref{sec:ident-theory}). With four decoders
we cannot tell which spacing better reflects how much each decoder uses brain
activity, and the difference in spacing does not show that BC contains
information that identification accuracy lacks for ranking decoders.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What BC Measures---and What It Does Not}

BC is a variance ratio, not a proportion of the image that is brain-controlled.
A value of $\mathrm{BC}=1.87$ does not mean that 87\% of a reconstruction comes
from the brain. It means that, in the chosen embedding space, the variance of
reconstructions across trials of the same stimulus would be 87\% larger if the
trial--stimulus pairing were random; equivalently, stimulus-driven variance is
about 0.87 times the within-stimulus variance, and the ICC is about 0.46
(\cref{eq:icc}).

BC measures consistency, not correctness, and not provenance. A high BC means
that the brain signal reliably steers the output for a given stimulus. It does
not mean that the output resembles the stimulus, for which identification
accuracy and perceptual similarity remain necessary. Nor does it mean that most
of the image comes from the brain: in the prototype example of the
Introduction, BC is very high although the generator supplies every detail.
Category-controlled BC separates out only the consistency explained by the
chosen categories.

BC contains no information that $F$, the Calinski--Harabasz index or the ICC
lack, and on NSD it ranks decoders as identification accuracy does. Its use lies
elsewhere. First, $\mathrm{BC}=1$ is a fixed null value regardless of the
design, and $\mathrm{BC}-1$ does not depend on the number of trials
(\cref{sec:god-metrics}), so values can be compared across studies with
different numbers of repetitions, provided the stimuli and the representation
are the same. Second, BC needs only trial labels. It can
therefore be computed at the ridge output or the backbone of a pipeline, where
no ground-truth target exists (although values at different stages describe
consistency in different representations), and it can be decomposed into brain-, category-
and sampling-driven parts, which identification accuracy cannot. Third, it can
space decoders differently from identification accuracy (\cref{sec:nsd-ident}),
although in our data the two never ordered decoders differently and we cannot
say which spacing is more informative.

\subsection{Stimulus-Specific Consistency in Current Decoders}

A natural expectation is that diffusion-based decoders, with priors trained on
billions of images, would produce outputs that barely depend on the stimulus
($\mathrm{BC}\approx1$) even from genuine brain data. Our NSD results do not
support this expectation for the decoders we tested: single-trial
reconstructions of MindEye2, MindEye1 and Brain-Diffuser all have BC between
1.25 and 1.87, and consistency that 40 semantic clusters do not explain
remains. This does not mean that the prior contributes little to the images; a
decoder can be highly consistent while the generator supplies most of the
details. What BC shows is that the dependence of the output on the stimulus is
a matter of degree, and that this degree differs between decoders and training
regimes. Reducing subject-specific training data lowers BC at
every stage after the ridge regression in every subject, and in each pipeline
BC is lower in the final image than in the token-averaged CLIP-like latents. A
simulation on GOD in which decoded features are mixed with shuffled features
shows the same graded behavior: BC decreases monotonically with the mixing
weight (\cref{fig:prior} in \cref{app:god}).

The seed decomposition qualifies how single reconstructions should be read. In
MindEye2 (subject 1, 100 images), the seed accounted for 55--63\% of the
within-stimulus variance of a single reconstruction in the embedding spaces we
evaluated. As a result, $\mathrm{BC}(1)-1$, measured from one sample per trial,
is only 37--45\% of $\mathrm{BC}(\infty)-1$, the value without sampling
variability. A single reconstruction should be read as one draw from a
distribution in which the seed contributes more within-stimulus variance than
differences between trials. Whether this holds for other decoders remains to be
tested.

\subsection{Recommendations for Reporting}

For decoders evaluated on repeated presentations, we suggest reporting:
(i) BC with a shuffled control and stimulus-level bootstrap intervals, alongside
identification accuracy; (ii) BC at each stage of the pipeline, read as a
description of consistency in each representation, with comparisons between
models made at the same stage; (iii) BC from several seeds per trial, with
BC(1) and BC($\infty$) or the sampling share; and (iv) category-controlled BC,
with the definition of categories stated.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{No advantage in ranking.} In all our comparisons BC ranks decoders as
identification accuracy does. The GOD matched-accuracy result
(\cref{sec:god-roi}) is small and post hoc, and the difference in spacing on
NSD (\cref{sec:nsd-ident}) rests on four decoders. Demonstrating a case in
which BC changes a conclusion drawn from identification accuracy remains open.

\textbf{Consistency is neither correctness nor provenance.} A decoder that is
consistently wrong can have high BC, and a hallucination that is reproduced
across presentations counts as brain control. BC does not measure how much of
an image comes from the brain rather than from the prior, and category control
is relative to the chosen clustering. BC should be reported together with a
reference-based measure, not instead of one. Because NSD test images share categories with the
training images, BC also does not test generalization to unseen categories,
which is central to the concern of \citet{shirakawa2025}.

\textbf{Repeated presentations are required.} BC needs at least two trials per
stimulus. It cannot be computed for designs with single presentations, and its
precision depends on the number of stimuli. We treat trials as independent;
session- and run-level effects are not modeled.

\textbf{Scope of the NSD experiments.} MindEye2 was evaluated in four subjects,
MindEye1 and Brain-Diffuser in subject 1 only, and the category and seed
analyses in subject 1 only. Final images were evaluated on 500 images (414 for
the method comparison) and the seed decomposition on 100. We did not evaluate
MindEye2's refinement stage or other recent methods. MindEye1 was run without
its best-of-16 selection, and Brain-Diffuser's regressions were retrained, so
absolute values may differ from the original pipelines even though the
reproduction checks matched.

\textbf{Dependence on the representation.} Image-space BC is measured through
DreamSim and CLIP embeddings, which are themselves trained on natural images and
emphasize some image properties over others. BC values depend on this choice
and, like the ICC, on the heterogeneity of the stimulus set, so ``calibrated''
here means a fixed null value and independence of the number of trials, not
comparability across stimulus sets. BC values are not directly comparable
across stages, datasets or feature spaces; the
GOD values (\texttt{cnn8}, 1.13--1.26) and the NSD values cannot be compared
directly. The category share depends on how categories are defined (here
k-means in CLIP space, and on $k$).

\textbf{Linear decoder on GOD.} The GOD analyses use a Ridge regression decoder
and AlexNet features; a more expressive decoder could yield higher BC.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

Visual quality and target similarity do not characterize the reliability of
reconstructions across repeated presentations. The Degree of Brain Control
measures this reliability. It equals 1 when the mean output does not depend on the stimulus, is an affine function of
the ANOVA $F$ statistic and an exact transform of the I2C2 estimate, and has a
scale that does not depend on the number of trials. On GOD, it separates real
from shuffled input in decoded features. On NSD, single-trial reconstructions
of MindEye2, MindEye1 and Brain-Diffuser all show stimulus-specific
consistency; less subject-specific training data lowers BC; about 45\% of the
final-image BC excess is explained by 40 semantic clusters; and, in MindEye2,
more than half of the within-stimulus variability of one reconstruction comes
from the sampling seed. BC ranks decoders as identification accuracy does, and
it does not tell how much of an image comes from the brain rather than from the
prior. Its value is as a calibrated, label-only reliability diagnostic that
complements identification accuracy and separates stimulus, trial, seed and
category contributions.
